title: Bird Trapping — Ingenious Capture Devices
url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bird_trapping
keyword: trapping

Capturing the serins, goldfinches, and sparrows in Cotán's painting required ingenious engineering. In 17th-century Spain, common methods included the "red" (net), "liga" (birdlime), and "trampa" (spring trap).

Birdlime was a sticky substance made by boiling holly bark (Ilex aquifolium) or mistletoe berries. Applied to branches where birds perched, it trapped their feet with an adhesive strong enough to resist wing beats but gentle enough to avoid injury. Goldfinches were especially vulnerable — their fondness for thistle seeds (hence "carduelis") made baited lime-sticks irresistible.

The "costilla" (rib-trap) used a balanced lever mechanism: a flat stone propped on a stick, with bait underneath. When a bird pecked the bait, the stick displaced and the stone fell — a simple machine unchanged for thousands of years.

Net trapping ("paranza") arranged nets between trees in a V-formation, funneling flying birds into an ever-narrowing space. Sparrows, being gregarious flock feeders, were particularly susceptible to this communal capture method.
