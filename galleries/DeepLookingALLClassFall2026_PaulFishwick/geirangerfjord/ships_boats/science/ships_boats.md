Normally, air gets colder as you go up, and warm exhaust rises and spreads out. On still days in a deep fjord, the opposite can happen: cold air sinks into the bottom of the valley, and a warmer layer sits on top like a lid. This is called a temperature inversion. Smoke and exhaust from ships get trapped under that lid instead of rising away, and the steep walls keep the wind from clearing them. That is one reason scientists measure air quality in Geiranger when many cruise ships are in the fjord.
