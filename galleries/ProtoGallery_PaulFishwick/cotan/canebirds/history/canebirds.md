Six birds threaded on a cane was a standard market unit in 17th-century Toledo. Municipal ordinances regulated how small game was presented for sale: birds on canes with heads visible for species identification, allowing buyers to count and inspect before purchase.

The pairing — two of each species — was not accidental. Sellers grouped birds by species pairs, and a cane of six (three pairs) represented a typical household purchase: enough for a single meal for a small family, roasted whole over coals.

Small-bird hunting was a subsistence activity for the poor. Boys and peasants caught serins and sparrows with lime-sticks and simple net traps in fields and orchards. Goldfinches required more skill — their wariness and smaller flock sizes made them harder to catch, which is why even dead goldfinches commanded a higher price than sparrows.

The Prado's identification of exactly two of each species suggests Cotán painted from direct observation of an actual market purchase, not from artistic imagination. This documentary precision characterizes all his bodegones — they are inventories as much as artworks.
