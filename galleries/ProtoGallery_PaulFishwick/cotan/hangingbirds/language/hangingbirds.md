The two hanging birds are a European Goldfinch ("jilguero") and a House Sparrow ("gorrión"), as identified by the Museo del Prado. "Jilguero" comes from Latin "carduelis," meaning "thistle-eater" — the same root as "cardoon," the great vegetable arcing upward on the right side of this very painting.

"Gorrión" derives from a pre-Roman Iberian root, suggesting the house sparrow's intimate association with human settlements predates recorded history on the peninsula. In Cotán's Toledo, sparrows nested in the walls of houses and churches — they were the most familiar of all birds.

The verb "colgar" (to hang) is used in Spanish market language for birds displayed on strings. "Colgados" (hung ones) distinguished individually strung birds from those sold on canes — a distinction in presentation that implied different freshness and quality.
