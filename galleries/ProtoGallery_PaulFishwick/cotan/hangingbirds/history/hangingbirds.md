title: Individually Hung — A Mark of Quality

In 17th-century Spanish markets, how a bird was presented signaled its quality and price. Birds hung individually on strings — like the goldfinch and sparrow here — were considered premium offerings compared to those bundled on canes.

Individual hanging allowed the buyer to inspect each bird's condition: plumpness, feather quality, absence of damage. It also permitted better air circulation during aging, producing more evenly conditioned meat. Market regulations in Toledo specified that birds sold "colgados" (hung) must be fresh enough that their eyes were still clear.

The goldfinch, despite its tiny size, was particularly valued. Its bright plumage made it a popular cage bird, and captured goldfinches fetched higher prices alive than dead. A dead goldfinch in a still life thus carries a double meaning — it represents not just food but a living beauty now stilled.

For Cotán, about to enter the Carthusian monastery in 1603, these individually hung birds may represent the singular, personal pleasures he was preparing to renounce.
