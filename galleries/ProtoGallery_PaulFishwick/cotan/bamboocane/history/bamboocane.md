The bamboo cane with skewered birds documents a specific commercial practice of early 17th-century Toledo. Small game birds — finches, thrushes, and sparrows — were sold in markets threaded onto canes for easy counting, transport, and display.

This practice was regulated by municipal ordinances. Toledo's market laws specified how birds should be presented for sale: strung on canes with heads visible for species identification. The buyer could count and inspect the birds before purchase. A cane of five birds, as Cotán depicts, was a standard market unit.

The practice connects to Spain's complex hunting traditions. Small-bird hunting with nets and lime-sticks was a common occupation for peasants and boys, providing protein to supplement grain-based diets. The Carthusian monastery Cotán would soon enter maintained its own bird-catching grounds — though as a lay brother on a vegetarian diet, he would no longer partake.
